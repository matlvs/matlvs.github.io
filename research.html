Research interests

Physics of living matter: how flows, mechanics and transport shape biological form and behaviour.

Adaptive flow networks and memory in Physarum polycephalum

The slime mould Physarum polycephalum is a single cell that forms a network of tubes in which cytoplasm is pumped back and forth by rhythmic contractions. Without a brain, it finds food, avoids hazards and keeps a memory of past stimuli encoded in the diameters of its tubes.

I study how this behaviour emerges from physics. Using flow-network theory, micro-rheology, reaction–diffusion models and quantitative microscopy, I look at how active fluids find paths through networks, how networks prune themselves into efficient transport structures, and how distributed sensing lets the organism escape unfavourable environments.

Physics of biological cavities

Many tissues build fluid-filled cavities, called lumens. One of the earliest examples is the blastocoel of the mammalian embryo. During my PhD at the Collège de France, I developed theories for how such cavities form and how their volume is controlled.

With experimental collaborators, we showed that the mouse blastocoel forms through hydraulic fracturing of cell–cell contacts, followed by the coarsening of many micro-lumens into a single cavity. I then developed a hydro-osmotic coarsening theory of cavity formation and, more recently, an embedded pump–leak model in which ion pumping, electro-osmosis and para- and trans-cellular transport together set lumen and cyst volume.

Self-assembled vasculature and lumen formation on-chip

Engineering functional blood vessels in vitro requires control over how endothelial cells open and connect lumens. In a collaboration with Dr. F. Mirzapour (TUM), funded by a TUM Interdisciplinary Life Science fellowship, I combine in-vitro and in-silico approaches to understand lumen formation in vascular tissue on-chip, and how electrochemical cues can guide the self-assembly of vascular networks.
